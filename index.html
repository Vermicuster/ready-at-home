Overdose happening? Call 911

Overdoses often happen at home. Keep naloxone in yours.

Naloxone is a nasal spray that can temporarily reverse an opioid overdose. Ontario pharmacies give it out for free, with no prescription and no health card. Learn the signs, pick up a kit, and know the five steps before you ever need them.

Know the signs of an opioid overdose

If someone shows any of these signs, treat it as an overdose. Naloxone won't harm them if you turn out to be wrong.

Can't be woken up

Doesn't respond to their name, a shake of the shoulders or a firm rub on the chest.

Slow or no breathing

Breathing is very slow, uneven, or has stopped.

Blue or grey lips and nails

On darker skin, check the lips, gums and inside of the eyelids.

Choking, gurgling or snoring

Deep snoring or gurgling can mean the airway is blocked. It is not someone sleeping it off.

Cold, clammy skin

Skin feels cool and damp to the touch.

Tiny pupils

The dark centre of the eye is very small, like a pinpoint.

Five steps that can save a life

Learn them now so you don't have to think in the moment. Every kit also comes with printed instructions.

  1. Shout and shake

    Say their name loudly, shake their shoulders and rub the middle of their chest hard. No response? Move on right away.

  2. Call 911

    Say the person won't wake up or isn't breathing. Put the phone on speaker and keep going. The law protects you.

  3. Give naloxone

    Lay them on their back and tilt the head back. Place the tip in one nostril and press the plunger firmly.

  4. Breathe for them

    Give 30 chest compressions and 2 rescue breaths, if you're comfortable doing so. Use the barrier in the kit.

  5. Repeat and stay

    No change after 2 to 3 minutes? Give the second dose in the other nostril. Stay until help arrives: naloxone wears off and the overdose can return.

Calling 911 won't get you charged for having drugs

Canada's Good Samaritan Drug Overdose Act protects people who call for help during an overdose. It covers the person who calls, the person who overdosed, and anyone still there when help arrives, even if the caller has left.

It protects you from

  • Charges for having drugs for your own use
  • Charges for breaking conditions about simple drug possession in pre-trial release, probation, a conditional sentence or parole

It does not protect against

  • Outstanding warrants
  • Producing or trafficking drugs
  • Other crimes
Read about the Act on Canada.ca

Get a free naloxone kit near you

Participating Ontario pharmacies give out free nasal spray kits and show you how to use them. You don't need a prescription or an Ontario health card.

Not every pharmacy keeps kits in stock, so call ahead before you go.

  • Free for anyone who could respond. That includes people at risk of an overdose and their family and friends.
  • Up to two kits a day from a participating pharmacy, for four doses in total.
  • Check the expiry date printed on the spray, and swap the kit for a new one before then. Any Ontario pharmacy will take back an expired kit.
  • Store it where everyone can find it, but out of reach of children and pets. Tell your household where it is.

    Mostly in private settings

    Most overdose deaths happen in private places like homes, so family and roommates are often the first ones there. That's who this campaign is for.

    An Alberta study found 23.9% fewer deaths for every 10,000 naloxone kits in circulation.

    Common questions

    Can naloxone hurt someone who isn't having an opioid overdose?

    No. It's a safe medication that won't cause more harm, even if the person took something other than opioids. If you're not sure, give it.

    Why call 911 if naloxone already worked?

    Naloxone only works for a short time and the opioid can last longer, so the overdose can come back. Paramedics need to check the person. Stay with them until help arrives.

    Who should keep a kit at home?

    Anyone who takes prescription opioids or uses other drugs, and the family and friends of someone at risk. Because the street supply is so toxic, people using drugs for the first time are at risk too.

    Do I need a prescription or a health card?

    No. The pharmacist may ask for your Ontario health card, but they can still give you a kit if you don't have one or would rather not show it.

    Will the pharmacist show me how to use it?

    Yes. Participating pharmacies train you on how to recognize an overdose and use the kit when you pick it up. Our workshops add hands-on practice.

    Is there a version of this in French?

    Every kit comes with instructions in English and French, and one of our online workshops runs in French.

    Need help now?

    All three are free and open 24 hours a day.
